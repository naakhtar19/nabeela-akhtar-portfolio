Overview

The Problem: A Broken Procurement Loop

Clemson University's asset purchasing ran entirely through email. A single purchase could require 5–8 back-and-forth communications before an order was even placed — with no structured form, no status visibility, and no compliance tracking. Once items arrived, staff manually entered every detail into DATS (Departmental Asset Tracking System), creating error-prone inventory records.

The system was accessible only to a handful of staff (Martin Clark and Trae King), leaving faculty, students, and PhD employees completely dependent on personal follow-ups. In stakeholder interviews, over 60% of users reported dissatisfaction with the overall experience. The mission: design a DATS-compatible interface that navigates the full lifecycle of a purchase request — from initiation to delivery — while automating invoice inputs and reducing workload by 80%.

Problem Space

Four Core Friction Points

Semi-structured interviews across four stakeholder groups — faculty members, university staff, system administrators, and PhD employees — revealed consistent, compounding pain points. Each role experienced the system differently, but every group identified the same root failures.

📧

Email-Dependent Requests

All purchase requests were submitted via unstructured email to Shared Services. A typical request required 5–8 rounds of communication to validate account numbers, department info, and item specifications before an order could even be placed.

🔍

No System-Level Transparency

Status tracking was person-to-person, not system-to-user. Users relied on Martin Clark personally keeping them updated. There was no way for a faculty member or PhD student to check where their request stood without sending yet another email.

📋

Fully Manual Data Entry

Every invoice was manually entered into DATS after item arrival. Staff maintained their own private spreadsheets with details DATS couldn't capture — warranty info, device names, equipment type — because the system's display was suboptimal and information couldn't be shared cross-department.

🏛️

Ownership & Compliance Gaps

As a South Carolina state institution, equipment decommissioning required documented transfer back to the state. DATS had no mechanism for this. Computers were given away undocumented — leaving inventory records with equipment "no longer with us" and no record of where they went.

User Research

Listening Before Designing

Semi-structured interviews were the deliberate choice here — because the system was poorly understood even by many of its own users. Faculty and students often thought DATS handled everything; staff restricted answers to DATS's narrow existing scope. A rigid script would have missed both. Adaptive probing uncovered the real picture.

After interviews, an importance survey ranked 18 identified needs on a 1–5 scale using Ulrich & Eppinger's technique. The results were decisive: 17 of 18 needs averaged a score of 4 or higher. Two needs were flagged as latent — unexpected or new — by 3+ respondents each.

Why Semi-Structured Interviews?

A key challenge: many stakeholders had limited or zero exposure to DATS and could barely recall the process. A rigid survey would have returned incomplete data. Semi-structured interviews allowed the team to probe, redirect, and extend into adjacent systems — extracting insights that a checklist would have buried.

Latent Needs Discovery

Any need marked as "unexpected or new" by 3+ survey respondents was labeled a latent need. Two emerged: facilitating inter-entity communication for advice, and enabling lending of Clemson-owned inventory. Neither was part of the original scope — both made it into the final design.

Final Design

Four Core Screens — One Unified System

Concept 6 emerged from rigorous screening and scoring — a weighted score of 4.28/5 against Tradogram and other leading procurement tools. The prototype was built in Axure RP using Clemson University's orange, white, and grey palette.

Screen 1

Home — Smart Recommendations

Search bar with real-time product recommendations based on purchase history. Scrollable repeater cards surface relevant items. Recently purchased items shown for quick re-order.

Screen 2

Purchase Request Form

Unified form with account splitting, ownership transfer toggle, estimated delivery date, and live price total. Replaces 5–8 emails with a single structured submission.

Screen 3

Order Summary Dashboard

Centralized hub showing order ID, date, department, product name, quantity, status, and invoice. Admins can approve or deny requests via dropdown — full transparency for all parties.

Screen 4

Order Tracking

Prominently displayed tracking link alongside shipment status, estimated delivery time, and live updates. Users no longer need to contact Martin — the system tells them.

Reflections

What This Project Taught Me

Semi-Structured Interviews Unlock What Surveys Miss

Many stakeholders had so little DATS exposure they couldn't complete a structured questionnaire. By adapting in real-time — probing into adjacent systems like Cherwell, following threads about state compliance — we uncovered two latent needs that became core features. A rigid survey would have returned blank answers.

Define "System" Before You Ask About It

A critical early finding: faculty thought DATS handled everything; admins thought we were only asking about DATS's existing features. Clarifying the definition of "system" at the start of each interview was essential to getting responses that were actually comparable and useful across stakeholder groups.

Importance Surveys Turn Intuition into Direction

The score-based importance survey — using Ulrich & Eppinger's 1–5 technique — transformed qualitative interview themes into a ranked, defensible priority list. Knowing that policy compliance scored 5/5 while tracking scored 4/5 meant every future design tradeoff had a data foundation, not just a team opinion.
